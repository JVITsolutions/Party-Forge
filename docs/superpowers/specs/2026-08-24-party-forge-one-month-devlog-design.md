# Party Forge One-Month Devlog Design

**Status:** Draft for owner review

**Target runtime:** 15-18 minutes, with permission to end earlier when the story is complete

**Primary editor:** CapCut

**Development cutoff and final gameplay capture:** August 28, 2026

## Purpose

Create the first complete Party Forge devlog: a game-first account of what was built during the first month, how an almost entirely new developer used an AI-assisted workflow to build it, what Party Forge is now, and what comes next.

The video has two equally important jobs:

- Sell the central fantasy of Party Forge to survivor-like, ARPG, autobattler, and build-crafting players.
- Make the development journey compelling to viewers interested in an unusually ambitious first month of AI-assisted indie development.

This is not a technical tutorial, an exhaustive changelog, or an exaggerated success story. It is a paced transformation story supported by real gameplay and honest limitations.

## Date and Milestone Language

The repository's first commit was created on July 29, 2026. For this video, August 28 is the locked 30-day development cutoff. Continue polishing through that milestone, then capture the definitive current-build gameplay used for the opening, thumbnail, feature proof, and closing. The calendar-date anniversary is August 29, so narration and publication can occur after the capture without changing the video's one-month framing.

## Audience, Tone, and Runtime

The primary audience is players who already understand the appeal of games such as Brotato, Vampire Survivors, and Soulstone Survivors. The secondary audience is people following AI-assisted game development or a beginner learning publicly.

The tone is grounded and conversational, with energetic gameplay bursts and occasional jokes. The host should sound proud of the progress without pretending the prototype is farther along than it is. The pace should remain brisk; 15-18 minutes is a ceiling rather than a quota.

## Core Message

The game's central pitch is:

> What if, instead of collecting a pile of weapons in a survivor-like, every "weapon" was a complete party member with their own class, gear, stats, and build?

The video's recurring visual and verbal shorthand is:

> YOUR PARTY IS YOUR BUILD

The development-story premise is that a lifelong player with nearly zero game-development experience became the game's director, designer, and lead playtester while using AI agents for much of the implementation, testing, and technical investigation.

The video must explicitly avoid implying that playing many games automatically makes someone a developer. The credible claim is that years of playing games helped build taste, pattern recognition, and ideas to test; the month of hands-on work is the beginning of learning how to turn those ideas into a game.

Show a short sequence of the host, a real development conversation, and the game running. Resolve on the title card.

Voiceover:

> So, with almost no game-dev experience and an AI development team, I spent a month trying to build it.

## Why I Started: 0:40-2:15

This section is primarily on camera, supported by short B-roll overlays.

Cover these points in this order:

1. The host first picked up a controller at four years old on the SNES and has remained passionate about games, now with more than a thousand games in the Steam library.
2. Previous development experience was limited: dozens of Blender tutorials and a couple of hands-on hours, roughly one or two hours experimenting in Unreal Engine 5 and importing assets, and a small amount of Python.
3. State plainly: playing games does not automatically make someone a developer. It does provide a large library of examples, strong preferences, and ideas that can be tested and iterated.
4. The project came from playing many survivor-likes—especially Brotato, Vampire Survivors, and Soulstone Survivors—and wanting one that felt like commanding and building a team instead of wielding a collection of weapons.
5. Nearly ten small projects were attempted during the month. Party Forge was the one whose central idea was worth pursuing as a real game.

Use images or very short clips rather than remaining on a static talking head. Suitable B-roll includes an SNES controller, a Steam library view that does not expose private information, early Blender work, the Unreal editor, project folders, and Party Forge's earliest footage.

## From Nothing to Playable: 2:15-5:00

Frame the 56-hour checkpoint as a question: could the core party idea survive contact with gameplay?

Show the original footage without apologizing for its roughness. Identify what already existed—movement, companions, enemies, automatic attacks, and the beginnings of upgrade choices—then contrast each with a matching current clip. The point is not that the early build looked good. The point is that it produced enough fun and enough unanswered questions to justify continuing.

The section should end on the transition:

> Once the core idea worked, the question stopped being "Can I make a prototype?" and became "What would this need to become an actual game?"

## Building Real Foundations: 5:00-10:30

Present development as five questions rather than a feature inventory. Each chapter gets a short setup, visual proof, and one sentence explaining why it matters to the player.

### 1. Can the Core Idea Work?

Use the 56-hour prototype as the origin point. Show early movement, enemy density, the first party behavior, and the crude but playable loop. Keep this recap brief because the preceding section has already established the footage.

### 2. Can Party Members Feel Like Characters Instead of Weapons?

Show several of the nine current classes: Fighter, Ranger, Mage, Cleric, Frost Mage, Marksman, Paladin, Rogue, and Warlock. Focus on visible differences in roles, attacks, positioning, upgrades, traits, stats, and the down/revive behavior.

Avoid presenting nine mini class guides. A rapid montage should prove variety, while two or three selected examples explain the design goal: a party slot is a character and a build commitment, not merely another projectile.

### 3. Can a Run Create Real Builds?

Show level-up choices, targeted upgrades, ranks, critical chance and multi-crit, damage types, defenses, and the Character Ledger. Use one short build story instead of isolated menus—for example, choose a class upgrade, show the relevant stats change, and then show the combat consequence.

The takeaway is that the game is building systems that can support deliberate party composition and scaling rather than random visual noise.

### 4. Can Progress Matter After a Run?

Show profiles, the menu, passive progression, inventory, equipment, the stash, comparison UI, and persistent loadouts. Make clear that ownership and extraction foundations exist, while the complete extraction loop does not.

The player-facing reason is that a party should feel owned and developed across runs, not discarded when a timer ends.

### 5. Can Prototype Scaffolding Start Becoming a Game?

Show the replacement of the earliest placeholder presentation with animated humanoids, attack animations, equipment, the rat enemy, modular gear, loot chests, and improved UI. Include a concise Blender or asset-pipeline comparison where it clarifies the transformation.

State honestly that much of the art is still temporary and the game has no implemented sound yet. Editorial music and transitions belong to the video, not to the game.

## The AI Development Team: 10:30-13:00

This section explains the process just far enough to make the development claim credible. Every tool shot should resolve into a gameplay result. Do not linger on scrolling code or present the segment as a prompt tutorial.

### Roles

The host is the game director, designer, and lead playtester. The host decides what the game should feel like, directs visual and audio goals, approves designs, and evaluates builds. AI agents handle much of the implementation, automated testing, technical investigation, and documentation.

Do not say the AI made the game by itself. Do not imply that the host manually wrote systems that were primarily agent-implemented. The interesting story is the collaboration and the decision loop.

### Tool 1: Codex as the Development Team

Show one real Party Forge conversation and the corresponding result. The visual process card is:

`IDEA → QUESTIONS → DESIGN → IMPLEMENTATION → TESTING → PLAYTEST`

Explain that important features begin with discussion and approval. The agents investigate the real project, propose a design, implement it, run tests, and return a build for human playtesting and direction. Use a concrete feature from the video as the example.

### Tool 2: Blender and the Asset Workflow

Show the path from placeholder to an animated result in Godot:

`PLACEHOLDER → BLENDER/ASSET WORK → ANIMATION CHECK → GODOT RESULT`

This can use the rat, a humanoid attack, or modular equipment—whichever has the clearest before-and-after footage. Explain only the parts necessary to understand how a visual idea becomes a tested in-game asset.

### The Broader Month

End with a very brief montage showing that nearly ten projects were attempted during the month. Party Forge is the project selected for sustained development, not the only experiment that existed.

## Where the Game Is Now: 13:00-14:30

Use the exact label `PLAYABLE SYSTEMS PROTOTYPE` on screen. Do not call the current build a demo, beta, vertical slice, or complete game.

What currently works:

- Nine playable classes with distinct combat roles.
- Party combat, positioning, and formation behavior.
- Leveling, traits, upgrades, statistics, and multi-crit foundations.
- Profiles and passive progression.
- Generated loot, inventory, equipment, comparison UI, and persistent loadouts.
- Ownership and extraction foundations.
- Keyboard, mouse, and controller-oriented interfaces.

What is not complete:

- Much of the art and presentation is temporary.
- Balance and numbers are still changing rapidly.
- The full repeatable run loop is not finished.
- The game has no implemented sound yet.
- Extraction, trading, campaign-scale content, multiplayer, and commercial polish remain future work.

Pair every statement with direct visual proof or a restrained text card. Avoid a long checklist spoken over unrelated gameplay.

## Where It Is Going: 14:30-17:00

Present the roadmap as an ordered direction, not a release promise.

1. Replace more placeholder presentation with production-ready characters, equipment, effects, sound, and stronger game feel.
2. Add party status interactions and cross-character synergies so composition changes how the whole team behaves.
3. Complete a repeatable longer run structure, currently envisioned around 30 minutes, with recurring bosses and a meaningful extraction decision.
4. Build local split-screen first, use Steam Remote Play Together as an early distribution option, and consider native online multiplayer later.

The multiplayer point must be framed as sequencing and ambition, not as a near-term feature promise.

Anchor the roadmap with:

> The goal isn't just another survivor-like with followers. I want every person in your party to be a build—and every combination of people to create a different team.

## Closing: 17:00-17:30

Return to the strongest current gameplay and, if it reads cleanly, briefly echo the earliest footage.

Recommended closing script:

> One month ago, this was an idea and a rough prototype. Month one was about proving the idea could work.
>
> The next stage is about giving Party Forge an identity: better characters and equipment, stronger effects and sound, deeper interactions between party members, and a complete run worth playing again.
>
> Out of the nine current classes, which four would you put in your party—and what interaction would you want them to have?
>
> If you want to see what someone with almost no game-dev experience can build with an AI development team, subscribe and follow the project.
>
> This is Party Forge after one month. Let's see what month two looks like.

Use a 10-15 second end screen with:

- Subscribe target.
- The Day 5 video.
- A looping current-gameplay background.
- `BUILD THE PARTY` as the final text.

Do not ask viewers to wishlist the game until a real store page exists.

## CapCut Visual System

Keep the edit achievable with six reusable patterns rather than bespoke motion graphics.

### 1. Gameplay Hero Shot

Use full-screen gameplay with a subtle 100%-to-106% keyframed push, a short text fade, and a hard cut on the music beat. Reserve this for the opening, major reveals, and the closing.

### 2. Before-and-Now Split

Stack the early and current clips, apply simple rectangular masks, and create a clean vertical divide. Use matching gameplay situations when possible so the improvement is easy to understand.

### 3. Title Impact

Use one bold text template, hold approximately two seconds, and enter or exit on a music beat. Reuse it for the title and major chapter cards.

### 4. On-Camera Honesty

Use direct-to-camera delivery, remove pauses with restrained jump cuts, cover cuts with relevant B-roll overlays, and add corrected auto-captions. Captions should support clarity without filling the frame with animated words.

### 5. AI Tool Demonstration

Use a screen recording with the host in picture-in-picture when helpful. Zoom toward the relevant question, decision, or result; do not make the audience read an entire conversation or code file.

### 6. Reusable Proof Card

Build one text-card compound clip for class counts, commit counts, milestone labels, and feature groups. Duplicate it throughout the project so typography and motion remain consistent.

### Style Rules

- Dark base drawn from Party Forge's presentation.
- Gold for the Party Forge identity.
- Orange for milestone labels and development timestamps.
- White text with a restrained shadow for readability.
- One primary transition style throughout the video.
- Prefer hard cuts, masks, scale keyframes, overlays, and clear typography over elaborate effects.
- Do not require After Effects or custom motion graphics.

## Audio Policy

The game itself currently has no implemented sound. The video may use licensed editorial music, narration processing, and restrained transition effects. It must not add fake attacks, impacts, loot sounds, or other effects that a viewer could reasonably mistake for current in-game audio.

When showing raw gameplay for more than a few seconds, briefly acknowledge the missing sound once, then let narration and music carry the section. Do not repeatedly apologize for it.

## Accuracy and Public-Claim Guardrails

- Recheck the exact day count and current commit count on the day narration is recorded.
- Prefer rounded claims such as `700+ COMMITS` when they remain true and easier to maintain.
- Describe features according to demonstrated current behavior, not the most ambitious design document.
- Label incomplete extraction, trading, campaign, multiplayer, and meta-progression work as foundations, goals, or future direction as appropriate.
- Never imply current in-game audio exists.
- Never imply the project has production-final art or balance.
- Present AI as a substantial implementation collaborator under human direction, not as an autonomous creator or a minor autocomplete tool.
- Keep private paths, account information, API keys, unrelated conversations, and unreleased personal information out of screen recordings.
- Use only footage, images, music, fonts, and effects that the channel has permission to publish.

## Production Success Criteria

The final video succeeds when:

- A new viewer can explain the party-as-build premise after the opening.
- The visual transformation is proven with real before-and-now footage.
- The development story remains understandable without a technical background.
- AI's role and the host's decision-making role are both represented accurately.
- Every major current-state claim has visible evidence.
- Incomplete systems and absent game audio are disclosed without overwhelming the pitch.
- The roadmap feels exciting but does not sound like a release-date promise.
- The final cut stays at or below 18 minutes and contains no section kept only to reach a target length.
- The audience receives one specific, easy-to-answer comment prompt.
